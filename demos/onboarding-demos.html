Easing into the atlas

Six ways to make the first thirty seconds survivable, each one playable below. Nothing here is implemented — this is for choosing. Every number shown is read from the live build (68 markers, 47 concerns, 6,192 follow-up tests); anything invented is labelled.

The problem, stated plainly: the atlas opens with 26 marker rows, 47 concern rows and every edge between them drawn at once. That is the honest picture, and it is also nobody's first question. The patterns below all work the same way — start from one thing the reader already has, and let the map earn its density.

01

One move at a time

The map starts almost empty. It holds your panel and a single instruction. Marking one result draws its own ripple and nothing else, so the density is something you built rather than something you walked into.

try it
why it works here
The emptiness is the tutorial — no overlay, no coach marks, nothing to dismiss. And it is true to the thesis: a blank middle column is an honest statement that nothing has been flagged yet.
what it costs
Someone with no results to hand sees an empty screen and leaves. Needs pattern 02 beside it.
02

Borrow someone else's bloodwork

Three one-click scenarios. You do not need your own labs to understand what the map does, and the reader who does have labs gets a worked example to compare against.

why it works here
Fastest path from arrival to comprehension, and the numbers it shows are real: the iron scenario lights four concerns whose follow-up lists run to 192, 103, 60 and 33 markers.
what it costs
A scenario must never read as a diagnosis. It needs a permanent label — example, not advice — and a one-click exit to your own data.
03

Guess before the reveal

The one genuinely game-like mechanic that earns its place. It teaches the single fact the whole product rests on — that one number opens onto many questions — by letting you be wrong about it first.

Calcium came back high.

How many of the 47 concerns can that one result point at, on its own?

why it works here
Curiosity gap, closed with real data in one interaction. Calcium is the strongest case in the catalogue at 9 concerns; the median marker reaches 1 — which is itself the more interesting lesson.
what it costs
One question is a hook; five is a quiz nobody asked for. Show it once, never again.
04

Three steps, then it goes away

Not a tour — a checklist that watches what you actually do and deletes itself when you have done it. The three steps are the three things the atlas can do.

    Done — this panel would now disappear and not come back.
    why it works here
    Cheapest of the six to build and the easiest to remove. Ticks respond to real actions, so it never blocks anything.
    what it costs
    It occupies the rail, which is where the markers live. Probably belongs in the footer strip that the legend now vacates.
    05

    Coverage, not score

    One honest number about the shape of your bloodwork: how much of the map it can even reach. The annual panel reaches 18 of 47 concerns. All thirteen panels reach 28. Nineteen are out of reach of blood entirely.

    18of 47 concerns your selected panels can reach
    The trap. A meter invites you to fill it, and "collect more panels" is a medical recommendation this tool has no business making. It works only if the number describes the map's coverage and never the reader — and if the 19 unreachable concerns are presented as a fact about blood tests, not a gap in you.
    06

    One ripple at a time

    Same map, two first frames. Left is what ships today. Right dims everything except one marker's ripple until you ask for more.

    today · everything at once
    focus · one ripple, rest at 15%
    Same rows, same positions, same edges — only opacity differs. No layout change, so nothing moves when focus turns off.
    why it works here
    Costs one CSS class and no layout work, and it obeys the existing rule that state is carried by colour and opacity rather than geometry.
    what it costs
    Dimming hides the thing the atlas is for — the surrounding context. Good as a first frame, wrong as a default forever.

    What I would not build

    Gamification has a standard toolkit, and most of it is actively wrong for a page about someone's blood.

    mechanicwhy not
    Points, XP, levelsRewards engagement with a medical record. The goal is that you come once, understand something, and leave.
    Streaks, daily check-insBloodwork changes on a scale of months. A streak would manufacture anxiety and reward re-reading old results.
    Badges for tests takenTurns ordering more tests into an achievement. Overtesting has real costs — false positives, incidental findings, money.
    Leaderboards, comparison to othersReference ranges are already population comparison, and they are age- and sex-dependent. A rank would be meaningless and frightening.
    A health scoreCollapsing 68 markers into one number is exactly the reduction this product exists to argue against.
    Confetti, celebration animationBreaks the no-ambient-motion rule, and there is nothing here to celebrate.
    Unlocking content by progressGating real clinical information behind a game loop. Never.

    If it were my call

    Combine 01, 02 and 04. The map opens nearly empty with the three-step strip in the footer and the three borrowed scenarios sitting where the concern column will be. Marking one result — your own or a scenario's — starts the reveal. That is an empty state doing three jobs at once and adds no new screen.

    Add 03 once, on the first result anyone marks, then never again. Hold 05 until the framing is settled; the meter is one word away from being a nudge to over-test. Skip 06 for now — 01 achieves the same calm without hiding anything.
